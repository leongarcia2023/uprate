When Do the Megawatts Arrive?

A public baseline for U.S. power uprate delivery timing

Summary

  • An increase to the licensed generation capacity of a nuclear power plant is called an “uprate.” DOE calls uprates and restarts its “fastest pathway” to new capacity.
  • The Department of Energy has set ambitious goals to leverage reactor uprates to meet unprecedented increases in demand for baseload power.
  • The NRC has approved 172 power uprates since 1977, totaling about 8 GWe. The last uprate approved was in November 2021, and nothing is under review today.
  • Extended power uprates (the most aggressive uprate, which typically requires substantial modification to the plant) cleared the review process in about 12 months in 2001 and 2002. Since 2005 they have taken a median of 23, which is a substantially slower pace. Measurement uncertainty recaptures also slowed from 7 months to 11.
  • Utilities have told the NRC to expect 31 uprate filings totaling about 2.4 GWe through 2032.
  • At historical review speeds, those filings put 0 MWe on the grid by the end of 2027 and 219 MWe by the end of 2029. If we factor in the three announced reactor restarts going according to schedule, about 50% of the 5 GWe UPRISE goal can be achieved by 2029. If the NRC hits the review targets it set for itself in 2024, then it’s 58%.

UPRISE aims for 2.5 GWe by 2027 and 5 GWe by 2029

DOE’s Utility Power Reactor Incremental Scaling Effort, UPRISE, wants 2.5 GWe of new capacity at existing nuclear plants by the end of 2027 and 5 GWe by the end of 2029. Both of these goals count uprates and restarts.

An uprate raises the thermal power a reactor is licensed to produce, and the NRC sorts them into three types:

  • Measurement uncertainty recapture (MUR), about 1.5%. More accurate feedwater flow meters let a plant reclaim part of the 2% margin it has for measurement error.
  • Stretch, up to about 7%, usually without major plant modifications.
  • Extended power uprate (EPU), up to 20%, typically involves installing new turbine and plant equipment.

Between a filing and new electrons on the grid are three steps: the NRC reviews the application, the NRC approves it, and the plant installs the change at a refueling outage. I looked and could not find a public dataset of how long each of those steps takes, so I built one from publicly available data and am sharing it here.

The NRC expects to receive 31 applications, totaling 2.4 GWe

Utilities inform the NRC of which uprates they plan to file and when. The bar plot below shows the NRC’s planned filing list as of September 8, 2026, by planned filing year, and the line is the running total. The dashed lines are the two UPRISE goals.

The whole list through 2032 comes out to 2,421 MWe, which is under half the 2029 goal before a single review starts. Seventeen of the 31 filings are expected in 2027, so 2027 and 2028 are when the NRC’s review capacity gets tested. The NRC’s pending list was empty as of September 15, so every megawatt on this chart still has the whole chain of review ahead of it.

The program has approved about 8 GWe in 45 years, and UPRISE asks for 5 GWe in 3

The NRC approved 172 uprates from 1977 to 2021, adding 24,146 MWt, which is about 8 GWe. When one looks at the record, three distinct eras emerge, with stretch uprates being the uprate of choice through 2000, the “EPU wave” of 2001 and 2002, and MURs and EPUs dominating review time after that. The last EPU was Browns Ferry in 2017, and the last approval of any kind was a Millstone 3 MUR in November 2021. Nothing has been approved since.

The entire program of uprating light water reactors has added 8 GWe in 45 years. UPRISE asks for 5 GWe of both uprates and reactor restarts in about 3 years.

164 review times, read from the NRC’s own letters

You shouldn’t take a dataset on faith, and in fact, all of the code and documentation to reproduce these findings is available here for download. Every publicly available NRC approval letter (available on the ADAMS database) cites the application it answers, usually in a sentence like “in response to your application dated March 24, 1977.” I wrote a simple text parser that pulls each letter from the NRC’s ADAMS document library and reads that date. The parser knows eight phrasings of the sentence, and the standard one matched 129 of the 147 dates it read.

The parser read 147 dates. I read 17 by hand due to poor scans and garbled text, letters that bundle several requests, and wording the eight variants don’t cover. Eight approvals from before 1997 have only microfiche era accession numbers and no ADAMS document to read, so they count among the 172 approvals but have no reported review time for the purpose of this dataset. Every row carries its ADAMS accession number, so you can check any date in about thirty seconds.

The eight phrasings

The parser tries these in order and takes the first match. Each one ends in a date like “March 24, 1977.” It ignores supplements, so the original application date is the one that counts.

In 2001 and 2002, EPUs cleared review as fast as MURs have since 2010

Each dot is one approval. Across is the approval date, and up is the review time, from the application date to approval. Click a dot to open its NRC letter.

The shaded band is the first EPU wave where nine EPUs were approved in 2001 and 2002, with reviews of 9.6 to 16.1 months and a median of 11.8. Right of the dashed line, 21 of the 22 EPUs approved since 2005 took 17 to 61 months, median 22.8. The exception is Ginna, a PWR, at 12.1 months in 2006. No BWR EPU approved since 2005 has cleared review in under 19 months. That shift follows the steam dryer failures at Quad Cities in 2002 and 2003, and the record dates the shift, but this is a correlation, not necessarily causation.

MUR reviews slowed down too, from a median of 7.0 months before 2010 (44 reviews) to 11.3 since (29). In 2001 and 2002, EPUs as large as 20% cleared review in about the time a 1.5% MUR has needed since 2010.

Highlight

Reviews have run about 2x the NRC’s 2024 targets

In a May 7, 2024 letter to the Nuclear Energy Institute, the NRC stated review targets of 6 months for a MUR, 9 for a stretch and 12 for an EPU. Its 2026 readiness slides (Transforming Readiness: Preparing the NRC for the Next Era of Power Uprates) count those months from the end of the acceptance review, which I take as about two months.

The dashed red lines are the targets and the black bars are what happened. Recent EPUs took a median of 22.8 months and recent MURs 11.3 months, which is just about twice the NRC’s targets. The one group that met today’s EPU target is the 2001 and 2002 wave at 11.8 months, so this pace has happened before.

My review times start at the application, but if you add the two month acceptance review to the targets, the gap shrinks. Recent MURs still run 41% over and recent EPUs 63% over this target.

Targets

The path from filing to first electrons

To turn review times into megawatts on the grid, I walk each filing on the NRC’s list through this chain. The filing date is the middle of the quarter the NRC lists. The review time is a random draw from real reviews approved since 2010, with 29 MURs with a median of 11.3 months and 14 EPUs with a median of 22.8. The NRC hasn’t approved a stretch uprate since 2008, so the two Salem stretch uprate filings draw from all 57 past stretch reviews, with a median of 10 months.

The plant then installs the change at a refueling outage ranging from 3 to 15 months after approval for a MUR or stretch, and 9 to 30 for an EPU, which often needs two outages. EIA generator records put the “step up” at Grand Gulf, Browns Ferry and Peach Bottom about a year after approval, and Nine Mile Point 2 at about two, in phases. Four plants can anchor those windows but can’t define a distribution, so the scenarios below move every window six months either way. I run the whole review chain 5,000 times per scenario.

When the megawatts arrive: nothing by 2027 and about half the 2029 goal

The plot below has an orange line indicating uprates at historical review times. The line is the median run, and the orange shaded band shows the middle 80% of runs. The dashed purple line is the median if the NRC meets its targets from the first filing. Dark blue shows the three announced restarts stacked on top of the uprates, with Palisades (about 800 MWe), Crane (835) and Duane Arnold (615) at company guidance as of September. The restarts never go through the review chain. The diamonds are the UPRISE goals.

By the end of 2027, uprates deliver 0 MWe at historical review times, and 17 MWe, one MUR, in the fastest case I model. The three restarts total 2,250 MWe, so the visible pipeline can’t reach the 2027 goal even if every restart lands on time.

By the end of 2029, uprates deliver 219 MWe, with the middle 80% of runs between 185 and 554. Add the restarts and the total is 2,469 MWe, 49% of the goal. If the NRC hits its targets, uprates give 662 and the total reaches 58%. Switch off a restart to see what a slip costs.

What goes into my estimate for 2029?

The median moves in lumps because each filing’s megawatts arrive at once. The 219 MWe is the small, early filings: the seven MURs and two Salem stretch uprates filed in the first half of 2027 each reach the grid by the end of 2029 in more than 80% of runs. The EPUs filed in 2027 make it in under 5% of runs. The Q4 2026 Constellation EPU carries all 335 MWe of the NRC’s 2026 total and makes it in 28% of runs. When it lands along with the small filings, 2029 reaches 554 MWe, the top of the band.

Recent news since the talk

On October 6, Google and Constellation announced a 20 year power purchase agreement tied to 890 MW of added capacity at 11 reactors in PJM: Braidwood, Byron, LaSalle, Quad Cities, Limerick and Salem. Constellation expects the first uprate to deliver by 2028. The NRC’s list already holds 12 unnamed Constellation filings plus the two Salem stretch filings, and the NRC hadn’t updated the list as of October 23. I can’t yet tell how much of the 890 MW is new to the list, or how much comes from turbine work that never needs an uprate review. The December refresh will check. Constellation’s date fits the model with a MUR filed in early 2027 reaching the grid by the end of 2028 in about 6 of 10 runs.

Which plants still have room?

Where could the next filings come from? Using only the approval record, I compare each unit’s total approved uprate with the 90th percentile for its reactor type: 20.0% for BWRs, across the 33 units with any uprate, and 11.7% for PWRs, across 68. The slashed part of each bar is the room left under that projection.

Perry and Columbia have some of the most room among BWRs, and both sit on the NRC’s list for EPUs. Brunswick is at the BWR line, and the list has it down for MURs only, which fits. This is by no means a perfect measurement, so one should treat it as a proxy where design margins, steam dryers and the turbine generator set the actual limit. The PWR line mostly reflects how few PWR EPUs the NRC has licensed. Beaver Valley and Vogtle are on the list for EPUs, and an EPU the size of any approved since 2005, 8% or more, would carry them past it.

Five uprate scenarios for 2029

I have five defensible scenarios for how this uprate program will go, and unfortunately none of them meet the goals set out by the program by that time. Installation six months later ends up reaching 49% of the goal. A typical installation timeline lands us at 49% as well. If we assume installation happens six months sooner, it’s 56%. If the NRC meets its review targets, we end up at 58%. If we meet all of the targets and achieve fast installation timelines, then we get to 64%.

None reaches the goal, and the reactor restarts constitute the vast majority of all of these. Uprates alone come to 4% to 19% of 5 GWe by 2029, before any supply chain limit enters.

The restart switches in the section above apply here too.

What does this mean for the uprate decision calendar?

  • Utilities. At historical review times, an EPU filed in 2027 is a 2030 or 2031 megawatt. The median runs 43 months from filing to power for an EPU and 21 for a MUR, so the outage that you book matters more than the approval date.
  • Suppliers. The demand signal exists, but installations cluster in 2030 and 2031, later than the UPRISE goals imply.
  • NRC. Hitting the 2024 targets is worth about 443 MWe by the end of 2029, and the 2001 and 2002 uprate wave shows that pace has been done before.
  • Program. Uprates unfortunately can’t reach the 2027 goal. The 2029 goal needs filings that aren’t on the list yet, MURs filed early, and restarts on time. Each restart that misses 2029 takes 615 to 835 MWe off the total.

Methods and limits

Review times

  • The date rule: take the original application date in the letter’s citing sentence, never a supplement. In a letter that bundles several requests, take the request that is the uprate.
  • For 76 rows, the accession in the NRC’s table points to an ADAMS package rather than a letter. The parser opened the package and found the letter for 73 of them, and I read the other 3 by hand. The dataset records which document each date came from.
  • A review time can run short, never long: when a utility withdrew and refiled, the approval letter cites the refiled application, so my clock starts there.
  • The 17 hand read rows carry a note in the dataset saying why the parser missed them and which judgment call I made, such as the three application dates cited for Crystal River 3 in 1981.

The delivery model

  • Each filing on the NRC’s list files at the middle of its listed quarter.
  • Historical reviews draw at random from approvals since 2010 for MURs (29) and EPUs (14), and from all 57 stretch reviews. The targets scenario draws (target + 2 months) times a uniform factor between 0.75 and 1.25.
  • Installation draws uniformly from 3 to 15 months after approval for a MUR or stretch and 9 to 30 for an EPU. The faster and slower scenarios shift both ends by 6 months.
  • Megawatts per filing: 17 for a MUR, about 1.5% of a typical unit, and 50 for a stretch, with the EPUs splitting the rest of each year’s NRC total. This is a large assumption which moves the 2029 number of MW more than any other.
  • Restarts are added on top at company guidance with 60% of 2,250 MWe in 2027 and all of it from 2028.
  • 5,000 runs per scenario with seed 7. The outcomes are discrete, so medians jump between a few values, and across seeds 1 to 20 the 2029 base median lands on 219, 226 or 236 MWe.

What the model leaves out

  • The runs treat each review as independent. A real slowdown would hit every filing at once, so the 80% bands are too narrow. The spread across scenarios, 49% to 64%, states the uncertainty better than any one band.
  • There is no review queue. The historical case assumes reviews keep their historical speed even with 17 filings arriving in 2027. For scale, the NRC approved 40 uprates in 2001 and 2002, including the nine fast EPUs.
  • Capacity that never needs an uprate review, such as a more efficient turbine at the same thermal power, sits outside the model and would add on top.
  • Megawatts follow the NRC’s own thermal to electric conversion. Supply chain limits on turbines, transformers and other long lead time equipment are not modeled here.
  • These are base rates, not a forecast for any one plant. Reactor type, steam dryer design and the completeness of the application matter much more and vary for a single unit.
